<!-- VISUAL: Red Sea, night, a container ship silhouette, a missile trail in the distance -->
On February 1st, 2024, somewhere in the Red Sea, a missile is arcing toward a
container ship called the Koi. It misses, barely. The ship isn't owned by a
government, and it isn't carrying weapons. It's chartered by a French
shipping company most people have never heard of, hauling the same boring
cargo it hauls every week: furniture, electronics, clothes, the stuff in
your house right now.

<!-- VISUAL: fast cuts of ports, cranes, a wall of containers -->
This video isn't really about the missile. It's about how that company, CMA CGM, quietly became one of the most powerful players in global trade,
and how a war that should have hurt it made it richer than it had ever
been. To understand how, we have to go back to a single ship, in 1978.

<!-- VISUAL: archival-style stills, Beirut, Marseille port, 1970s -->
Jacques Saadé was a Lebanese businessman who fled Beirut as civil war broke
out. He landed in Marseille with one ship, one route, and four employees, a line running between Marseille and Beirut, built to keep a link open to
the home he'd left behind. He called it Compagnie Maritime d'Affrètement.
CMA.

<!-- VISUAL: growth chart, ships multiplying, dates ticking forward -->
For the next two decades he grew it the way most shipping fortunes get
built: patiently, and through acquisition. In 1996 he bought Compagnie
Générale Maritime, the former state-owned French line that once ran
legendary transatlantic liners like the Normandie, a fading piece of
national pride, privatized and struggling. He turned it profitable within a
year, merged it with his own company in 1999, and instantly had the
twelfth-largest shipping line on Earth. By 2006, it was already third.

<!-- VISUAL: 2016 NOL/APL acquisition graphic, ship count increasing -->
Then the acquisitions stopped being about ships at all. In 2016, CMA CGM
spent 2.4 billion dollars buying Singapore's Neptune Orient Lines. Three
years later it bought CEVA Logistics, a freight-forwarding company
operating in over 160 countries, and that deal changed what the company
actually was. It didn't just want to move your container across an ocean
anymore. It wanted to own every step before and after, too.

<!-- VISUAL: logos assembling, CEVA, GEFCO, Ingram Micro, Bolloré, FedEx Supply Chain, planes -->
So it kept buying. A stake in France's largest last-mile delivery firm. An
automotive logistics company. Its own air cargo airline. 4.85 billion euros
for Bolloré Logistics. And, this year, 1.4 billion for FedEx's contract
logistics arm. Add it up and CMA CGM can now touch a shipment from a
factory floor in Vietnam to a doorstep in Ohio without handing it off to
anyone else, ever.

<!-- VISUAL: split screen, stock exchange ticker (Maersk) vs a family portrait silhouette (Saadé) -->
Here's the detail that makes all of it possible. Maersk trades on the
Copenhagen stock exchange and answers to shareholders every quarter. CMA
CGM doesn't answer to anyone, almost three-quarters of it is still owned
by the Saadé family. Jacques handed the company to his son Rodolphe in
2017 and died the following year. Every acquisition since has happened on
the family's own terms. No earnings call. No outside board to convince.

<!-- VISUAL: French news channel logos, BFM-TV, RMC, newspapers -->
And that kind of freedom is exactly how a shipping company ends up owning a
news channel. In 2024, Rodolphe Saadé spent 1.55 billion euros on BFM-TV,
France's most-watched news channel, plus the radio station RMC, two
newspapers, and a stake in the broadcaster M6. None of it has anything to
do with containers. What it did was put Saadé in direct competition for
French public opinion with Vincent Bolloré, another industrial tycoon who
owns the rival channel CNews. Two logistics billionaires, fighting over
French television, because when a company answers to no one but its own
family, there's nothing stopping it from becoming one.

<!-- VISUAL: map, Red Sea, Suez Canal, missile trajectory graphic, ship diverting around Africa -->
Which brings us back to that missile. Within hours of the strike on the
Koi, CMA CGM rerouted every one of its Red Sea services around the Cape of
Good Hope, adding up to two weeks and roughly a million dollars to every
single Asia-to-Europe voyage. Every major carrier followed within months.
Suez Canal revenue for Egypt collapsed from over ten billion dollars in
2023 to around four billion in 2024, as the world's busiest shipping
shortcut emptied out.

<!-- VISUAL: profit chart spiking upward against a backdrop of the same ships rerouting -->
And here's the part nobody saw coming: the crisis that disrupted a
chokepoint carrying twelve percent of global trade was extremely good for
CMA CGM's bottom line. Fewer usable routes meant less effective capacity.
Less capacity meant higher freight rates. Higher freight rates meant CMA
CGM's shipping division posted a fifty-two percent jump in profit that
year. A crisis for global trade. A bonanza for the company moving it.

<!-- VISUAL: return to the MSC/Maersk/CMA CGM comparison graphic, now updated -->
And CMA CGM isn't even the biggest winner. MSC, the actual largest
container line in the world, is also privately held, by the Aponte
family, who've run it since 1970, eight years longer than the Saadés have
run CMA CGM. Two Mediterranean shipping families now control more
container capacity than every publicly traded shipping company on Earth
combined. Maersk, the one name people actually recognize, isn't even close
to first anymore.

<!-- VISUAL: pull back to CMA CGM Tower, Marseille skyline -->
Back in Marseille, the tallest building in the city is the CMA CGM Tower, a swooping glass skyscraper built by a family that arrived from Beirut with
one ship and a single route. It's a fitting monument to a company that
grew, quietly, acquisition by acquisition, until it became one of the
handful of businesses actually running global trade, without most of the
world ever noticing.

<!-- VISUAL: closing wide shot, container ship at sea, fade to black -->
You've probably never shipped anything with CMA CGM directly. But there's a
good chance something in your house crossed an ocean in one of its boxes, you just never thought to check whose name was on the side. Now you will.
